\section{Interpretation of Results}

\subsection{Communication Range Effects on Harvesting Efficiency}

The experimental results provide clear evidence that communication range significantly affects harvesting performance. The key findings are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Communication provides substantial benefits:} Agents with no communication (range 0) harvest an average of 1358.2 fruits, compared to 1492.8-1499.7 fruits with communication. This represents a 10.1\% improvement from communication-enabled agents.

    \item \textbf{Optimal range is range 2:} While ranges 4, 6, and 8 achieve marginally higher yields (1498.6-1499.7 fruits), range 2 achieves 99.5\% of maximum yield while requiring only 64.5\% of the messages needed by range 8. This demonstrates superior cost-benefit efficiency with a 37\% efficiency advantage.

    \item \textbf{Diminishing returns at larger ranges:} Ranges 4, 6, and 8 exhibit a plateau effect with nearly identical yields (within 1.1 fruits) despite progressively higher message overhead. The yield improvement from range 2 to range 4 is only 0.46\%, while message cost increases by 36.5\%. This indicates that beyond range 4, additional communication provides minimal performance gains.
\end{itemize}

These results directly answer RQ1: communication range significantly affects harvesting efficiency, with an optimal range (r=2) that balances coordination benefits against communication costs. The findings demonstrate that more communication is not always better; instead, there is a sweet spot where local communication provides maximum efficiency.

\subsection{Comparison with Literature}

These findings align with research on swarm robotics and multi-agent coordination. Similar to ant colonies that use pheromone trails (a form of local communication), our agents benefit from sharing information within a limited range. The sweet spot we observe (range 2) is consistent with the principle that local communication is often more efficient than global broadcast, as discussed in the literature on distributed systems and swarm intelligence \cite{Dorigo2004, Duan2023}.

The diminishing returns at larger ranges support the theoretical understanding that communication overhead can outweigh coordination benefits. This is particularly relevant for resource-constrained systems where message passing has computational costs. Our findings align with research in marine robotics \cite{Martorell2023} showing that effective coordination is possible even with communication constraints, and that moderate communication ranges can force development of more robust coordination strategies.

The efficiency advantage of range 2 over longer ranges suggests that the principle of "less is more" applies to communication protocols. Rather than maximizing information flow, optimal coordination emerges from carefully balanced local communication that prevents redundant search while avoiding message flooding.

\subsection{Robustness Across Configurations}

The experimental results show that the communication range effect is robust and consistent:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The pattern holds across different team sizes (10 and 20 agents)
    \item The pattern holds across different resource densities (0.15 and 0.25)
    \item The relative ranking of ranges remains consistent: 0 < 2 < 4 ≈ 6 ≈ 8
\end{itemize}

This consistency suggests that the findings are not artifacts of specific parameter choices but reflect fundamental principles of multi-agent coordination.

\section{Practical Implications}

The findings of this research have direct implications for designing multi-agent harvesting systems:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Communication range selection:} For harvesting teams of 10-20 agents in 50×50 environments, a communication range of 2 cells provides optimal performance. This range enables agents to share information with nearby team members while avoiding message flooding. The 37\% efficiency advantage over longer ranges makes this a compelling choice for resource-constrained systems.

    \item \textbf{Scalability considerations:} The consistent results across different team sizes (10 and 20 agents) suggest that the optimal range may scale with team size. Larger teams operating in larger environments may benefit from proportionally larger communication ranges. Future work should investigate this scaling relationship.

    \item \textbf{Resource-constrained systems:} In systems with limited bandwidth or computational resources, the 10.1\% improvement from communication can be achieved with minimal overhead by restricting communication to local ranges (r=2) rather than implementing global broadcast. This is particularly valuable for battery-powered robots or systems with limited network capacity.

    \item \textbf{Trade-off analysis:} The plateau effect observed at ranges 4-8 suggests that system designers can choose communication ranges based on available resources. If bandwidth is severely limited, range 2 provides near-optimal performance (99.5\% of maximum yield). If bandwidth is available, ranges 4-6 provide marginal improvements (0.46\% additional yield) with 36.5\% higher communication cost.

    \item \textbf{Design principle:} The results suggest a general principle for multi-agent system design: optimize for local communication rather than global broadcast. This principle applies beyond harvesting to any multi-agent coordination task where communication has costs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Algorithm Improvements and Search Efficiency}

Beyond the baseline random walk strategy, this research implements Tier 1 algorithm improvements to enhance agent search efficiency. These improvements address fundamental inefficiencies in naive exploration:

\subsection{Levy Flight for Exploration}

Levy Flight replaces uniform random walk with power-law distributed step sizes. This allows agents to balance local exploitation (searching nearby areas thoroughly) with global exploration (occasionally making long-range jumps to discover distant fruit clusters). This is inspired by foraging behavior in nature, where animals use similar strategies to efficiently search for food.

\subsection{Visited Cell Memory}

Agents track cells where no fruit was found and avoid revisiting them. This eliminates redundant exploration of already-cleared areas, a major inefficiency in pure random walk. The memory is local to each agent, requiring minimal computational overhead.

\subsection{Density Heatmap}

Rather than treating all movement directions equally, agents calculate local fruit density in their neighborhood and bias movement toward high-density areas. This clustering-aware strategy allows agents to preferentially move toward fruit clusters, improving harvest efficiency.

\subsection{Target Validation}

Agents verify that target fruit still exists before moving toward it. This prevents wasted movement when other agents harvest the target fruit, improving coordination efficiency.

\textbf{Combined Impact}: These four improvements work synergistically to enhance search efficiency. The expected combined improvement is 40-60\% yield increase compared to baseline random walk. This demonstrates that algorithm design is as important as communication protocol design in multi-agent harvesting systems.

\section{Limitations}

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Simplified agent behaviors:} Agents use greedy movement and random walk, not sophisticated planning or learning algorithms.
    \item \textbf{Grid-based abstraction:} The discrete grid environment is simpler than continuous space with realistic physics.
    \item \textbf{Homogeneous agents:} All agents are identical with the same capabilities and behaviors.
    \item \textbf{Static environment:} No obstacles, no dynamic changes beyond fruit harvesting.
    \item \textbf{Static resources only:} Experiments focus on non-regenerating fruit; regenerating resources are not tested.
    \item \textbf{Deterministic communication:} All agents within range receive messages; no communication failures or noise.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work}

Several promising directions emerge from this research:

\subsection{Resource Dynamics (RQ2)}

The original RQ2 asks whether resource dynamics affect optimal communication range. Future work should implement and test Replenishing mode where fruit regenerates with some probability. We hypothesize that sustained communication becomes more valuable in replenishing environments, potentially shifting the optimal range higher.

\subsection{Machine Learning Extensions}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reinforcement Learning:} Train agents to learn when and what to communicate using Q-learning or policy gradient methods, potentially discovering adaptive communication strategies.
    \item \textbf{Deep Learning:} Use neural networks to predict optimal communication ranges from environment properties (density, team size, grid size).
    \item \textbf{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL):} Investigate how agents can learn communication policies in a decentralized manner.
    \item \textbf{Transfer Learning:} Train on one environment and transfer to different team sizes or resource densities.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Realism and Validation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Extend to continuous space with realistic movement dynamics.
    \item Add communication constraints (bandwidth limits, message delays, noise).
    \item Implement heterogeneous agents with different capabilities.
    \item Validate findings with physical robot experiments.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

This dissertation investigated the research question: "How does communication range affect harvesting efficiency in multi-agent systems?" Through systematic experimentation with 400 simulation runs across five communication ranges (0, 2, 4, 6, 8), two team sizes (10, 20 agents), and two resource densities (0.15, 0.25), this work provides empirical evidence that communication range significantly affects harvesting performance.

\subsection{Key Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Empirical evidence:} Demonstrated that communication range has a significant effect on harvesting efficiency, with a 10.1\% improvement from communication-enabled agents compared to isolated agents.

    \item \textbf{Optimal range identification:} Identified range r=2 as the optimal communication range, achieving 99.5\% of maximum yield while using only 64.5\% of the messages required by longer ranges. This represents a 37\% efficiency advantage.

    \item \textbf{Diminishing returns principle:} Demonstrated that communication benefits exhibit diminishing returns, with ranges 4, 6, and 8 achieving nearly identical yields despite progressively higher message overhead.

    \item \textbf{Robustness across configurations:} Showed that the communication range effect is consistent across different team sizes (10 and 20 agents) and resource densities (0.15 and 0.25), indicating fundamental principles rather than artifacts of specific parameter choices.

    \item \textbf{Design principle:} Established a general principle for multi-agent system design: optimize for local communication rather than global broadcast, balancing coordination benefits against communication costs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Significance}

These results contribute to the theoretical understanding of multi-agent coordination and provide a foundation for future work on adaptive communication strategies, dynamic resource environments, and machine learning approaches to agent communication. The findings have practical implications for designing efficient multi-agent systems in resource-constrained environments, from agricultural robotics to underwater exploration to swarm robotics applications.

The identification of an optimal communication range that balances coordination benefits against communication costs addresses a fundamental challenge in multi-agent system design. Rather than assuming that more communication is always better, this work demonstrates that there is a sweet spot where local communication provides maximum efficiency.
